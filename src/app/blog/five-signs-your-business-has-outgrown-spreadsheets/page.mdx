Spreadsheets are genuinely useful. They are inexpensive, flexible, and familiar to most teams. Many businesses start with them because they are the right tool for an early version of a process.

But spreadsheets don't fail all at once. They fail a little at a time, in lost hours and errors nobody catches, while everyone keeps saying things are fine. Here are the five signs I look for.

## 1. The spreadsheet has an owner, and everyone is afraid of them leaving

If there's one person who "knows how the sheet works," your business logic lives in that person's head. The formulas, the workarounds, the reason column Q is hidden: none of it is written down anywhere else.

At that point you're running an unmaintained software system with no documentation and a single developer who can quit.

## 2. People re-type the same information more than once

An order arrives by email. Someone types it into the order sheet. Then into the invoicing tool. Then into the shipping portal. Three chances to typo a quantity, and thirty minutes nobody gets back.

Re-keying is the clearest sign that your tools have stopped cooperating. Copying data between screens is exactly the kind of work computers should be doing instead of people.

## 3. Two copies of the truth disagree

The sales sheet says one number, the accounting export says another, and the month-end meeting starts with twenty minutes of figuring out which one to believe.

When people stop trusting the numbers, they stop using them, and decisions drift back to gut feel. You end up with all the cost of tracking data and none of the benefit.

## 4. "Can we see..." questions take days to answer

Which products actually make us money? Which customers are slipping away? Simple questions, but answering them means someone spends a weekend stitching exports together.

Reporting shouldn't be a project. When basic visibility takes that much effort, the tool has hit its limit.

## 5. The workarounds have workarounds

A tab called "FINAL v3 (USE THIS ONE)". A cell that must never be sorted. A macro nobody dares touch. Each workaround made sense at the time; together they mean the spreadsheet's structure stopped matching the business a long time ago.

## What to do about it

Not every one of these means "buy custom software." Sometimes the fix is an off-the-shelf tool. What these signs do mean is that it is time to compare the real cost of the spreadsheet, in hours, errors, and decisions made on bad numbers, with the cost of replacing it.

The right replacement is usually smaller than people fear: a focused tool that handles your workflow properly, well short of a full ERP implementation. Start with the single most painful process, fix that, and let the results argue for the next step.
